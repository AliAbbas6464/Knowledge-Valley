CMA Exam Dates & Testing Windows 2026: When to Book Part 1 and Part 2

CMA exam dates 2026 and the three IMA testing windows — Jan–Feb, May–Jun and Sep–Oct — with Prometric booking deadlines and when to book Part 1 and Part 2.

October 22, 2026 · 14 min read

The CMA exam dates 2026 look simple on a poster — three windows, pick one — and yet choosing a window is the decision candidates most often get wrong, usually because they pick a date first and build a life around it afterwards. In my research for this guide I worked through IMA's registration rules, the Prometric scheduling process, and the reschedule penalties in the CMA Handbook, and then I did the thing most date guides never do: I laid the 2026 windows over the actual working calendar of a finance professional in the Gulf and Europe — Ramadan, Eid breaks, fiscal year-end closes, audit season. A window that is perfect on paper can be a terrible idea in your real year. This article gives you the verified dates and deadlines first, then the strategy for choosing between them, so the date you book is one you can actually be ready for.

THE SHORT ANSWER — CMA exams run in three windows in 2026: 1 January–28 February, 1 May–30 June and 1 September–31 October. IMA registration closes on the 15th of each window's second month — 15 February, 15 June, 15 October — with no late entry. You book your day, time and Prometric centre; results arrive six weeks after your test month ends.

The 2026 Windows at a Glance

IMA offers the CMA exam during the same three testing windows every year, worldwide. Within a window, you may sit on any day the Prometric centre has a seat — there is no single global 'exam day'. Here is the complete 2026 picture, including the format change that makes this year's windows different from each other:

WindowTesting dates 2026IMA registration closesSecond-section format
Window 11 Jan – 28 Feb 202615 February 2026Essays (traditional format)
Window 21 May – 30 Jun 202615 June 2026Your choice: essays or case-based questions (CBQs)
Window 31 Sep – 31 Oct 202615 October 2026Case-based questions only

Two things in that table deserve emphasis. First, the registration deadline is not the day before the window closes — it is the 15th of the second month, and IMA does not offer late registration. Miss 15 June and the May–June window is simply gone for you; your next chance is September. Second, your registration is valid for one window only, and you cannot sit the same part twice within a single window. Register for a window you cannot realistically prepare for and you have not bought flexibility — you have bought a deadline with a fee attached. This is also why the format row matters: the window you choose in 2026 quietly chooses your exam format, a point I return to in the strategy section below.

How Registration and Prometric Booking Actually Work

Candidates often blur two separate steps into one, so let me separate them cleanly. Registering with IMA and scheduling with Prometric are different actions, with different organisations, and both must happen.

Step one is IMA. You join IMA as a member (professional, academic or student category), pay the one-time CMA entrance fee, and then register and pay for the exam part you intend to sit, choosing your testing window. That entrance payment starts an important clock: you have three years from entering the programme to pass both parts. After registering, IMA issues your authorisation — the letter/number that confirms you are eligible to test in that window.

Step two is Prometric. With your authorisation, you go to Prometric's CMA scheduling pages, choose your country and test centre, and pick your actual date and time slot inside your registered window. You receive a confirmation number — keep it; you will need it for any change and on exam day. The practical point I cannot stress enough: registering with IMA does not reserve you a seat. Seats are allocated when you schedule with Prometric, and they are finite. A candidate who registers in May but schedules in late June has spent six weeks exposed to everyone else's bookings. Register and schedule in the same sitting, ideally the same day.

Where You Can Sit: Prometric Centres in the Gulf and Europe

CMA candidates are well served in KV's core markets. In the UAE, Prometric testing for CMA runs in Dubai (the AMIDEAST centre at Dubai Knowledge Park is the long-standing location) and in Abu Dhabi. Saudi Arabia offers centres in Riyadh and Jeddah, with additional Prometric locations elsewhere in the Kingdom. Elsewhere in the Gulf, candidates sit in Doha (Qatar), Kuwait City (Kuwait), Manama (Bahrain) and Muscat (Oman). In Europe, Prometric centres operate in London and other major UK cities, and across continental hubs such as Paris, Frankfurt/Berlin, Amsterdam and Madrid, among many others.

Three honest caveats belong here. Centre lists and even specific addresses change — centres relocate, and availability varies by window — so treat your Prometric scheduling search, done with your authorisation number, as the only definitive list, and check it early. Some locations offer limited CMA seat days rather than daily testing, which is another reason late scheduling hurts. And if your city has no centre, build the travel into your plan from the start: a candidate flying from Khobar to Riyadh or from Sharjah to Dubai on exam morning is fine; a candidate discovering that fact in the final week is not.

The Seat-Scarcity Reality Nobody Warns You About

Every window has the same shape: comfortable availability at the start, and a squeeze at the end. Working professionals — most CMA candidates — overwhelmingly prefer the last two or three weeks of a window, because they want maximum study time. The result is predictable: late-window slots at popular Gulf centres, particularly Dubai and Riyadh, fill first, and the candidates who scheduled late are left choosing between an inconvenient date, a distant centre, or a different window altogether. I have seen all three outcomes, and none of them improves anyone's score.

The fix is boring and total: schedule the moment your authorisation arrives, and schedule for a date about a week before your 'must be ready' date, not on the window's final day. That buffer absorbs a work crisis, a family emergency or a centre reschedule without destroying your plan. Booking early is not eagerness; it is insurance you pay nothing extra for.

Rescheduling, Cancelling and Transfers: The Fees That Punish Hesitation

Life happens, and IMA/Prometric do allow changes — for a price, on a sliding scale that rewards deciding early. Under the policies set out in the CMA Handbook, the position within the same testing window works broadly like this:

When you actWhat happens
46+ days before your appointmentReschedule or cancel with Prometric free of charge
5–45 days before your appointmentReschedule/cancel permitted, with a Prometric fee (US$70 under current handbook policy)
Less than 5 days before, or no-showChanges not permitted — the full exam fee for that part is forfeited
Moving to a different testing windowA transfer, not a reschedule: cancel with Prometric, then submit IMA's Exam Switch Form and pay ICMA's transfer fee (US$150, non-waivable; one switch per exam part). Prometric's cancellation fee may also apply

Read the last row carefully, because it is the most misunderstood rule in the whole system. Cancelling your Prometric appointment does not move you to the next window — it only cancels. Moving windows is a separate ICMA process with its own form and fee, and it must be requested at least five days before your scheduled appointment. Candidates who assume 'I'll just push it to September' and cancel inside the final week often discover they have neither a seat nor a transfer — just a forfeited fee (currently US$545 per part for professional members) and a fresh registration to pay for. Confirm the exact fees in your IMA account at the time you act, because fee schedules are revised periodically; the structure above — free early, charged in the middle, forfeited late — is the durable part.

Which Window Suits Working Professionals in the Gulf and Europe?

Now the strategy. The best window is the one where your study hours survive contact with your job and your calendar, so let's be specific about 2026.

Window 1 (January–February) suits candidates who start preparing in autumn and want the traditional essay format, and it lands after most companies' calendar year-end close is finished — but only just. If your employer runs a January close, an audit, or annual budgeting through January, your final study weeks collide with the busiest fortnight of your working year. In the Gulf, Ramadan is expected to begin around 18–19 February 2026, so late-February sittings also mean testing while adjusting to fasting and shortened Ramadan working hours — manageable for many, but not a detail to discover late. The honest profile for Window 1: prepared early, lighter January workload, or both.

Window 2 (May–June) is the flexible one — the only window where you can still choose essays, or opt into case-based questions. In Saudi Arabia and parts of the Gulf, late May also brushes Eid al-Adha (expected around 26–27 May 2026, subject to moon sighting), and June brings European summer leave rotations in multinational teams. Study time across April–May is generally cleaner for Gulf finance staff once Q1 reporting settles. For candidates retaking a part they failed in Window 1, this is also the natural next attempt — the performance report from a January/February failure arrives in time to rebuild properly for May–June rather than rushing.

Window 3 (September–October) is, in my assessment, the kindest window for most working professionals in our markets. Preparation runs through the summer — quieter in many Gulf offices as colleagues rotate on leave, and after the European August shutdown — audit season and half-year closes are behind you, and the year-end storm hasn't started. The trade-off is format: by September, case-based questions are the only option, so your practice must be CBQ-shaped, and results landing in November–December sit close to year-end close for calendar-year companies. Candidates targeting 'CMA qualified by early 2027' should treat Window 3 as the anchor of their plan, with Window 1 of 2027 as the natural home for the second part.

Sequencing Part 1 and Part 2 Across the Year

You may take the parts in either order, and you do not need your result for one part before registering for the other — but wise sequencing uses the results calendar rather than ignoring it. Results arrive about six weeks after the end of your test month. So a candidate sitting Part 1 in early May receives the result around mid-July, leaving the entire September–October window for Part 2 with the result already in hand. Sit Part 1 at the end of June instead, and your result lands around mid-August — still workable for Window 3, but with less room to react if the news is bad.

My recommended pattern for a working professional starting now is one part per window, two windows apart at most: for example, Part 1 in May–June 2026 and Part 2 in September–October 2026 — qualified by the end of 2026, inside a single calendar year, without ever studying two syllabi at once. Attempting both parts in one window is legal and occasionally done by full-time students, but for anyone holding a finance job it usually produces two mediocre performances instead of one pass. And keep the master clock in view: both parts must be passed within three years of entering the programme, so 'I'll do Part 2 someday' needs a date on it, even if that date is a year away.

The Verdict: Book the Window Your Life Can Support

The CMA calendar rewards planners and taxes improvisers — the deadlines are fixed, the seats are finite, and the fees for changing your mind grow teeth inside the final six weeks. The candidates who qualify smoothly are rarely the ones who found the 'best' month in the abstract; they're the ones who matched a window to their workload, their format preference and their study hours, booked the seat the day the authorisation landed, and then simply kept the date. Choose deliberately, book early, and the calendar becomes your ally instead of your excuse.

Frequently Asked Questions

What are the CMA exam dates in 2026?

Three two-month testing windows: 1 January–28 February, 1 May–30 June and 1 September–31 October 2026. Within a window you choose your own day and centre when you schedule with Prometric.

What is the last date to register for CMA in 2026?

IMA registration closes on 15 February, 15 June and 15 October 2026 respectively — the 15th of the second month of each window. There is no late registration.

Can I take CMA Part 2 before Part 1?

Yes. IMA allows the parts in any order. Most candidates take Part 1 first because its planning and costing content underpins Part 2, but a strong background in corporate finance or decision analysis can justify starting with Part 2.

How do I book my exam with Prometric?

After registering and paying with IMA, you receive an authorisation. Use it on Prometric's CMA scheduling site to select your country, centre, date and time within your registered window, and keep the confirmation number you are issued.

What does it cost to reschedule a CMA exam?

Within the same window: free if done 46 or more days ahead, a Prometric fee (US$70 under current policy) between 5 and 45 days ahead, and not permitted inside 5 days — a no-show forfeits the full exam fee. Moving to a different window is a transfer via IMA's Exam Switch Form with a separate US$150 ICMA fee.

When do CMA results come out for each 2026 window?

Approximately six weeks after the end of the month you tested in. As a guide: January sittings report around mid-March, June sittings around mid-August, and October sittings around mid-December.

Which 2026 window is best for working professionals?

For most Gulf and Europe-based professionals, September–October offers the cleanest preparation run — after summer leave and half-year close, before year-end. May–June suits candidates who want the choice of essays or CBQs; January–February suits early preparers with a light January workload.
