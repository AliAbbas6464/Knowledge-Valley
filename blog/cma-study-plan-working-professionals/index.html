The CMA Study Plan for Working Professionals: Pass Both Parts on 8–10 Hours a Week

The CMA study plan for working professionals: pass both parts on 8–10 hours a week with a week-by-week timetable, topic-wise testing and revision cycles.

October 22, 2026 · 14 min read

Every CMA study plan for working professionals I found while researching this article failed the same test: it was written for someone who doesn't have a job. Twenty hours a week. Marathon weekend sessions. 'Just wake up at 4 a.m.' — advice that survives exactly one week of real life in a finance department in Dubai or Riyadh. So this plan starts from the opposite direction. It assumes you work full time, that month-end close will eat some of your evenings, that family and rest are not optional extras, and that what you genuinely have is 8–10 hours a week. The good news, backed by the arithmetic in this article: 8–10 honest hours a week is enough to pass both parts — the global pass rate of around 45–50% per part is not beaten by more hours, it is beaten by better-distributed ones. Here is the preparation plan, week by week.

THE SHORT ANSWER — Yes — you can pass both CMA parts on 8–10 hours a week. At that pace each part takes about 4–5 months: roughly 10 weeks learning the syllabus, 5 weeks of heavy question practice, and 3 weeks of mocks and revision, split between 1-hour weekday sessions and longer weekend blocks.

The Maths First: Why 8–10 Hours a Week Is Enough

Prep providers commonly cite 150–200 hours of study per part (the IMA advises at least 150), so a part needs somewhere between 16 and 22 weeks at 9 hours a week. Call it 18 weeks — a little over four months — for a typical working candidate with an accounting background. Two parts, taken back to back with a short breather between them, fit inside 9–10 months. That is the whole secret of this CMA study schedule: there is no secret. There is a total, a weekly rate, and a calendar — and the discipline to keep the rate ordinary but unbroken.

What makes 8–10 hours work, when 15 chaotic hours often fail, is distribution. Memory consolidates between sessions, not during them; five one-hour encounters with budgeting techniques across a week will beat one exhausted five-hour Sunday binge almost every time. The plan below is built on that principle: short weekday touches that keep the material warm, and one longer weekend block where the deep work — problem sets, essays, mocks — actually happens.

The Weekly Timetable: Your 9-Hour Week

Here is the sample CMA weekly study timetable this whole plan runs on. It assumes evening study after work on the UAE pattern (weekend Saturday–Sunday, as it has been since January 2022). If you are in Saudi Arabia or Qatar, where the weekend is Friday–Saturday, shift the two weekend blocks to Friday and Saturday and make Sunday your light review day instead — the structure matters, not the day names.

DaySessionLengthWhat happens in it
MonEvening, after work1 hrNew material: one sub-topic from the current section (video/notes first, then 10–15 questions)
TueEvening, after work1 hrQuestion practice on yesterday's topic + redo every question you got wrong last week
WedEvening — live class or self-study1 hrNew material: next sub-topic; end with a 10-question mixed mini-quiz from earlier sections
ThuOff (or 30-min flash review)0–0.5 hrRest, family, life. Optional: formula cards on the commute
FriOff / light0 hrFull rest day — this is deliberate, not a failure
SatWeekend deep block3 hrsThe week's heavy lifting: full problem sets, one timed essay/case practice, error-log review
SunWeekend second block2 hrsFinish the current section; cumulative MCQ set (30–40 questions); plan next week
Weekly total8–9 hrsRises to ~10 hrs in the final month of each part, when mocks replace regular sessions

Two rules keep this timetable alive. First, the weekday hour is sacred and small: one hour, same time, phone in another room — small enough that even a bad day at work rarely kills it. Second, a missed session is rescheduled, never doubled: trying to cram two hours tomorrow to pay for today's miss is how people start dreading the plan and then abandoning it. A week that delivers six of the nine hours is a fine week. Perfection is not the strategy; continuity is.

The Phase Plan: Learn → Practise → Prove It

Within each part, your weeks divide into three phases. The mistake I see most often in self-made plans is spending 90% of the time in Phase 1 — reading and watching — and arriving at the exam having never performed under exam conditions. This split fixes that.

PhaseWeeks (per part)Share of your hoursWhat you actually do
1 — LearnWeeks 1–10~50%Work through the syllabus section by section in weight order (see below). Notes or classes first, then 15–25 questions per sub-topic. Goal: full syllabus covered once, nothing skipped.
2 — PractiseWeeks 11–15~30%Question-bank heavy: 100+ MCQs a week in mixed sets, timed. Every wrong answer gets a written reason in an error log. First two essay/case practices under time. Weak sections get a second pass.
3 — Mock & reviseWeeks 16–18~20%Two to three full 4-hour mock exams in real conditions. Revision is now driven entirely by mock results and your error log — no new material after week 16.

Notice what the phases do to your confidence problem. By week 15 you have already answered well over a thousand practice questions on that part — the widely recommended target across prep providers is roughly 1,500–2,000 MCQs per part across the whole preparation, and this plan's mix of daily questions, weekend sets and mocks lands you in that range without ever having a 'question marathon' week. Volume is not the goal in itself; it is how technique and timing become automatic, so that on exam day your brain spends its energy on the questions, not on the format.

Study in Weight Order: Let the Syllabus Percentages Plan Your Weeks

Not all sections deserve equal weeks. The IMA publishes exactly how each part is weighted, and a smart CMA preparation plan spends its scarcest resource — your evenings — in proportion. In Part 1, Section B (Planning, Budgeting and Forecasting) and Section C (Performance Management) carry 20% each — together, 40% of your exam. The other four sections (External Financial Reporting, Cost Management, Internal Controls, Technology and Analytics) carry 15% each. In Part 2, Section C (Decision Analysis) alone is 25%, with Financial Statement Analysis and Corporate Finance at 20% each, Professional Ethics at 15%, and Risk Management and Investment Decisions at 10% each.

Translated into the Learn phase, that means roughly this for Part 1's ten weeks: two weeks each on Budgeting and on Performance Management, about a week and a half each on the four 15% sections. For Part 2: two and a half weeks on Decision Analysis, two each on Financial Statement Analysis and Corporate Finance, one and a half on Ethics, and a week each on Risk and Investment Decisions. One caution: weight is not the same as your personal difficulty. If your day job is budgeting, Section B may need half the time the table gives it — and Internal Controls, which you never touch at work, may need double. Sit a diagnostic set of questions in week one, and let your own scores bend the plan. The weights set the default; your error log corrects it.

Essays, Mocks and the Final Two Weeks

The essay (case-based) section frightens candidates out of proportion — partly because many plans ignore it until the end. Don't. Remember the mechanics: you must answer at least half of the 100 MCQs correctly to unlock the two essay scenarios, and then you have one hour for both. The fix is integration, not a last-minute module. From Phase 2 onwards, write one timed essay a week inside your Saturday block — scenario, structured answer, calculations shown, a clear recommendation at the end. Graders reward structure and justified conclusions, not volume. By exam day you will have written eight to ten of them, and the format will hold no surprises.

Mock exams follow a simple cadence: the first full mock at the end of Phase 2 (treat it as a diagnostic, expect a humbling score), then one a week through Phase 3, each sat in a single four-hour sitting, at the same time of day as your real booking, with no pausing the clock. Review each mock for as long as you sat it — the learning lives in the review, not the score. As a readiness guide, candidates scoring consistently in the mid-70s percent and above on reputable provider mocks are generally in passing territory; treat any single mock as information, not a verdict.

The final two weeks have their own rules. No new topics — anything not learned by now is a calculated sacrifice, and there is always something. Taper the hours slightly rather than cramming (a tired brain underperforms a rested one by more than one extra chapter can add). Revisit your error log and formula sheets daily in short bursts. In the last 48 hours: one light review, exam-day logistics confirmed (Prometric location, ID, arrival time), an early night. And book nothing at work for the morning after, if you can avoid it — you have earned the slow morning.

A Worked Example: Ahmed's Part 1, Week by Week

Ahmed is a financial analyst in Riyadh, married, two children, working the Saudi Sunday–Thursday week. His weekend blocks sit on Friday and Saturday; his employer runs a hard close in the first week of each month. Here is how his Part 1 actually unfolded on this plan.

Weeks 1–4 (Learn): External Financial Reporting and the first half of Budgeting, at eight hours a week — he skipped weekday sessions during close week and used his Friday block for double problem sets instead. Weeks 5–10 (Learn): Performance Management, Cost Management, Internal Controls and Technology & Analytics, one section roughly every ten days, with a Knowledge Valley live class twice a week anchoring his weekday hour. End of week 10: syllabus fully covered once, about 700 practice questions banked.

Weeks 11–15 (Practise): mixed timed sets, his error log now the boss of the plan. It told an uncomfortable story — variance analysis (Section C) was leaking marks — so week 14 became a variance-analysis repair week instead of the planned general review. Two timed essays written. Weeks 16–18 (Mock & revise): three full mocks scoring 68%, 74% and 78%. He sat the real exam in the third week of the window, passed, and took a full week off before Part 2. Total elapsed: four and a half months, average just under nine hours a week, zero all-nighters. His verdict afterwards is the one I hear most: 'The plan was boring. That is why it worked.'

Built for Gulf Working Life, Not Against It

A plan survives contact with a real Gulf working life only if it is scheduled around that life from day one. A few adjustments experienced candidates swear by: study before Fajr or immediately after work rather than late at night — willpower is a morning resource; in Ramadan, shrink to maintenance mode (question practice and revision only, 30–45 minutes after taraweeh) and add a catch-up week after Eid instead of pretending Ramadan weeks are normal weeks; and tell your manager and your family the plan and the exam date early — the candidates who hide their CMA attempt get no protection for their evenings, and unprotected evenings are where plans go to die.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I really pass CMA studying only 8–10 hours a week?

Yes. Each part needs roughly 150–200 total study hours, so at 8–10 hours a week you cover a part in about 4–5 months. Most working professionals who pass study in exactly this range — consistency over 18 weeks beats intensity for two.

How many practice MCQs should I do per part?

Prep providers widely recommend working through roughly 1,500–2,000 practice MCQs per part, including mock exams, and reviewing the explanation for every wrong answer. It is provider guidance, not an IMA rule — quality of review matters more than the raw count.

What should my weekly CMA study schedule look like?

One hour on three or four weekday evenings (new material and question practice), plus a 3-hour weekend block for problem sets and essays and a 2-hour block for cumulative revision — about 9 hours total, with at least one full rest day.

When should I start taking mock exams?

Take your first full mock at the end of the practice phase — around week 15 of an 18-week part plan — then one a week in the final 2–3 weeks, each in a single timed four-hour sitting with a full review afterwards.

How do I fit essay (case-based) practice into the plan?

From the practice phase onwards, write one timed essay every week inside your long weekend session — structured answer, calculations shown, clear conclusion. Eight to ten essays before exam day is enough to make the format routine.

How do I study during Ramadan or month-end close?

Switch to maintenance mode: 30–45 minutes of question practice and revision only, no new material, and add a catch-up week afterwards. During close week, protect just the weekend blocks. Plans that bend survive; plans that demand normal weeks in abnormal months break.

Should I study Part 1 and Part 2 together or one at a time?

One at a time. Working professionals get better results focusing all 8–10 weekly hours on a single part, sitting it, then starting the second part within two or three weeks while study momentum is still warm.

Final Verdict

Pin this above your desk if it helps: passing CMA on a working schedule is not a talent contest, it is a logistics exercise — 150–200 hours per part, delivered in unglamorous one-hour instalments, aimed by the syllabus weights, rehearsed under exam conditions, and bent (never broken) around close weeks and Ramadan. An 8–10 hour week, held for nine or ten months, has carried accountants, analysts and finance managers across the Gulf and Europe to the CMA letters for years. Your week is enough.
