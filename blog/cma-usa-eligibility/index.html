CMA USA Eligibility in 2026: Degree, Experience and Membership Requirements, Explained

CMA USA eligibility in 2026 explained: the degree requirement and your options without one, two years of work experience, IMA membership and timelines.

October 22, 2026 · 14 min read

Every month, I speak to finance professionals across Dubai, Riyadh and a dozen European cities who ask me the same question in different words: 'Am I even allowed to do CMA?' Behind it sits a cluster of myths — that you need an accounting degree, that you must finish two years of experience before you may sit the exam, that a degree from outside the US somehow doesn't count. Almost all of it is wrong, and the wrongness costs people years. So I went back to the source — the IMA's own CMA Handbook and certification pages — and rebuilt CMA USA eligibility from the primary documents, rule by rule. This guide covers the degree rule (and the professional-certification escape hatch), the experience rule in all its detail, the membership and fee mechanics, the two clocks that run on every candidate, and what you owe IMA after you certify. If you are checking whether CMA is open to you, this is the page to check against.

THE SHORT ANSWER — CMA USA eligibility has three pillars: (1) a bachelor's degree in any field from an accredited institution — or an IMA-approved professional certification instead; (2) two continuous years of professional experience in management accounting or financial management, which you may complete within seven years after passing; and (3) active IMA membership, the one-time entrance fee, and passing both exam parts within three years of entering the programme.

The Three Pillars at a Glance

Strip away the handbook language and CMA certification rests on exactly three pillars, plus a conduct requirement that runs underneath all of them: you must comply with IMA's Statement of Ethical Professional Practice. Nothing on this list requires US citizenship, US residency, or an accounting degree — three facts that surprise a large share of the Gulf and European professionals I talk to. The CMA is a genuinely global credential: you can study in Dubai, test in Riyadh, hold a degree from a university in Berlin or Lahore, and gain your experience in any country, because IMA's requirements are about what you know and what you have done, not where you did it.

Pillar 1 — The Education Requirement: Any Degree, Properly Accredited

The education rule is broader than most people expect: a bachelor's degree in any field, from an accredited college or university. IMA says 'any field' and means it. I have seen CMA candidates whose first degrees were in engineering, computer science and pharmacy — the qualification was designed for people who run the internal finances of companies, and those people do not all arrive through accounting faculties. What IMA polices is not the subject of your degree but the standing of the institution that awarded it: the college or university must be accredited.

For degrees earned outside the United States — which means most readers of this article — the mechanism is credential evaluation. IMA accepts degrees from institutions recognised through the relevant international frameworks, and where there is any doubt about equivalency, you will be asked for an independent evaluation from an approved agency (the evaluation bodies IMA points candidates to are members of organisations such as NACES or AICE). My practical advice from walking candidates through this: do not leave the evaluation until after you pass. If your university is not a well-known name in the US system, get the evaluation done while you study. It is inexpensive, it removes the one administrative risk in the whole process, and you will likely need the same document again one day for an employer or an immigration file. A master's degree, by the way, also satisfies the requirement — the rule is a bachelor's or higher.

One timing subtlety that matters enormously for students: you do not need the degree in hand to sit the CMA exams. IMA lets candidates test first and submit proof of graduation within seven years of passing. That single clause is what makes CMA a realistic final-year university move instead of a post-graduation project — a point I return to in the scenarios below.

No Degree? The Professional-Certification Route

Here is the least-publicised line in the CMA requirements: the bachelor's degree can be replaced by a professional certification that IMA approves. The certifications IMA recognises for this purpose include the CFA charter, the CIA (Certified Internal Auditor), the CFE (Certified Fraud Examiner) and the CTP (Certified Treasury Professional), and ACCA members likewise qualify — IMA waives the degree requirement for members of approved professional bodies, with ACCA the most common case I encounter in the Gulf and Europe, where plenty of senior accountants built their careers on professional qualifications rather than university degrees.

Two cautions before you rely on this route. First, the list and its conditions are IMA's to change, and IMA verifies the credential directly with the issuing body — expect to have an official letter or verification sent from the certifying organisation to IMA. Second, the waiver replaces only the degree. You still need the two years of qualifying experience, both exam parts, and active membership. Used correctly, though, this route is exactly what a 38-year-old ACCA-qualified finance manager in Jeddah with no university degree needs — and in my experience, most such professionals have never been told it exists.

Pillar 2 — The Experience Requirement: Two Years, Properly Understood

The experience rule is where eligibility questions get serious, because IMA's definition is specific: two continuous years of professional experience in management accounting and/or financial management. The handbook's test is functional, not titular. Your job title does not decide it; your duties do. Qualifying experience means work in which you regularly make judgements applying management-accounting and financial-management principles. IMA's own list of qualifying work includes preparation of financial statements, financial planning and analysis, monthly/quarterly/year-end close, auditing (external or internal), budget preparation and reporting, managing the general ledger and balance sheets, forecasting, company investment decision-making, costing analysis, and risk evaluation.

Just as important is what does not count. Internships and trainee positions do not qualify. Clerical and non-technical work — bookkeeping data entry, accounts-payable processing, routine transaction posting — does not qualify, because it does not require professional judgement. Roles that only occasionally touch accounting principles (sales, general operations, IT support) do not qualify either. The honest summary I give candidates: if your two years could be described as 'processing' rather than 'deciding and analysing', IMA will probably not accept them, and it is better to hear that from me now than from ICMA after you have passed both parts.

That seven-year window deserves emphasis, because it reverses the sequence most people assume. You may pass both exam parts as a fresh graduate with zero experience, then complete your two qualifying years afterwards — you simply aren't a CMA, and can't use the letters, until the experience is verified. For Gulf professionals this is strategically valuable: exam success makes you dramatically more employable for exactly the FP&A and management-accounting roles that satisfy the requirement, so the credential helps you earn the experience that completes the credential.

Pillar 3 — Membership, Entrance Fee, and the Three-Year Clock

Before you can register for an exam part, two administrative steps must be done: join IMA, and pay the one-time CMA entrance fee to enter the certification programme. At IMA's current published rates (revised September 2025), a professional member pays US$295 for annual membership and a US$300 entrance fee, then US$545 per exam part — roughly US$1,700 in IMA fees across the whole journey, before study materials. Students pay substantially less (about US$1,090 all-in at IMA), because student membership is priced for people still at university. The practical routes into membership are therefore two: the student route, for those currently enrolled (cheapest, and the reason I push final-year students toward CMA), and the professional route, for everyone already working. Membership must stay active throughout your candidacy — let it lapse and you complicate your exam registration and, later, your certification.

Entering the programme starts the clock that candidates underestimate: you must pass both parts within three years of entering. Pass one part and drift, and the pass expires with the window. In practice three years is generous — coached, working candidates typically finish in 12–18 months — but the clock converts 'someday' into a deadline, and it is why I tell every candidate to enter the programme when they are ready to study, not months before. The exams themselves run in three testing windows a year (January/February, May/June, September/October) at Prometric centres worldwide — including across the Gulf and Europe — and the English-language exam is the global standard version (IMA also administers the exam in Chinese and Japanese in those markets). Each part is four hours: 100 multiple-choice questions plus two case-based scenarios, with a scaled pass mark of 360 out of 500.

After You Pass: Certification Is a Subscription, Not a Trophy

Passing both parts and verifying your degree and experience earns the certificate — and starts your obligations as a CMA. Every certified member must complete 30 hours of Continuing Professional Education (CPE) each year, including at least two hours of ethics, keep IMA membership current, and pay the annual CMA maintenance fee (currently US$30). CPE is self-reported through your IMA profile, certificates are kept in case of audit, and up to 10 excess hours can carry into the next year. Fall behind and your status turns inactive — you may no longer hold yourself out as a CMA until you cure the shortfall, and long lapses can eventually require far more than paperwork. None of this is onerous for a working finance professional — employer training, IMA webinars and conferences cover most of it — but candidates should walk in knowing the letters carry an annual maintenance rhythm, exactly as ACCA's CPD or any serious credential does.

Three Readers, Three Verdicts

Eligibility rules only become clear when they meet real CVs. Here are three I see constantly in the Gulf and Europe:

  • The fresh graduate in Dubai, degree in hand, no experience yet: Eligible — via the test-first route. Your degree (any field) satisfies the education pillar once IMA accepts the institution, and you can sit both parts in your final year under the student membership rate. The two experience years come afterwards — target FP&A or management-accountant roles in Dubai's free zones, and your certification completes itself as you work.
  • The experienced accountant in Riyadh whose degree is not in accounting or finance: Eligible — on duties, not degree relevance. The unrelated degree is no obstacle: any bachelor's counts. Your years in Riyadh count if the work involved judgement — budgets, costing, close, analysis — rather than pure processing. Bring your job description, not your job title, to the verification.
  • The European finance graduate weighing CMA against local qualifications: Eligible — twice over. The degree satisfies the education pillar outright, European experience counts in full, and if your route was a professional qualification instead of a degree, the IMA-approved certification waiver covers you. Your only real questions are timing and study hours, not permission.

Frequently Asked Questions

Who can do CMA USA — what is the minimum eligibility?

Anyone with a bachelor's degree in any field from an accredited institution (or an IMA-approved professional certification instead), who joins IMA, pays the entrance fee, passes both exam parts within three years, and completes two years of qualifying experience within seven years of passing. There is no nationality, residency or age condition.

Can I do CMA without an accounting or finance degree?

Yes. The degree requirement is discipline-blind — engineering, science and humanities graduates are all eligible. You will need to build the assumed foundations (financial accounting, economics, basic statistics, time value of money) during preparation, but eligibility itself is not in question.

Can I sit the CMA exam before completing my degree or experience?

Yes to both. IMA allows candidates to test first and submit proof of education within seven years of passing; the two years of experience can likewise be completed within seven years after passing. You are certified only when every pillar is verified.

Does a degree from outside the USA count for CMA?

Yes, provided the institution is accredited/recognised. IMA may ask for an independent credential evaluation (through approved evaluation agencies) to confirm US bachelor's equivalency — arrange it early if your university is lesser-known internationally.

What work experience counts — and what doesn't?

Work requiring regular judgement in management accounting or financial management: FP&A, budgeting, costing, close, financial reporting, internal/external audit, investment decisions, risk evaluation. Internships, trainee roles, and clerical/processing work do not count. Part-time work of ~20+ hours a week counts pro-rata.

What does CMA eligibility cost before I even sit an exam?

IMA membership plus the one-time entrance fee: at current rates, US$295 + US$300 for professionals (US$595 to enter); students pay considerably less. Exam parts are then US$545 each. Verify current fees at imanet.org before registering.

What must I do to keep the CMA after I earn it?

30 CPE hours per calendar year including at least 2 hours of ethics, active IMA membership, the annual CMA maintenance fee, and compliance with IMA's Statement of Ethical Professional Practice.

Final Verdict

Read the rules as IMA wrote them, and CMA eligibility turns out to be one of the most open gates in professional finance: any accredited degree, experience that can follow the exams, a certification route for those without a degree, and no geographic conditions at all.
