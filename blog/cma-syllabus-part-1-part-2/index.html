CMA Part 1 vs Part 2 Syllabus: Every Section, Topic Weight and What to Study First

CMA syllabus compared in full: every Part 1 and Part 2 section, topic weights A to F, IMA coverage levels and what to study first for maximum marks.

October 22, 2026 · 13 min read

Most CMA candidates open the syllabus the way tourists open a menu in a language they half-speak — everything looks important, so everything feels equally urgent, and they start at page one and study in order. It is the single most expensive mistake in CMA preparation, and it comes from not knowing one thing: the CMA syllabus is not a flat list. It is a weighted map. The IMA publishes, in its official Content Specification Outlines (CSOs), exactly what share of each exam each section carries — and once you can read that map, your study plan writes itself. I have rebuilt both parts below directly from the IMA's CSO document: every section A to F, its official weight, the topics inside it, and — the part most guides skip — an honest strategy for what to study first, built on the weights and on how the topics lean on each other. Whether you are in Dubai, Riyadh or Rotterdam, this is the map to plan against.

THE SHORT ANSWER — Part 1 (Financial Planning, Performance, and Analytics) weights Planning, Budgeting & Forecasting and Performance Management highest at 20% each, with the other four sections at 15% each. Part 2 (Strategic Financial Management) is led by Decision Analysis at 25%, then Financial Statement Analysis and Corporate Finance at 20% each, Professional Ethics 15%, and Risk Management and Investment Decisions 10% each.

How to Read This Map: Weights, Sections and Levels

Each CMA part is a four-hour exam — 100 multiple-choice questions plus two case-based scenarios — scored on a 0–500 scale with 360 to pass. The CSO divides each part into six sections, labelled A to F, and assigns each section a coverage percentage: the approximate share of the exam drawn from that section. A 20% section will contribute roughly twenty of your hundred MCQs; a 10% section, roughly ten. IMA is explicit that no weights are assigned inside a section — the topics within a section carry no official ranking, and you should not infer importance from the order of the list. The weights I use throughout this article are the official CSO values, unchanged in the current outlines and confirmed against IMA's own regional certification pages.

The CSO also assigns every section a coverage level — and here the message is bracing: all twelve sections, in both parts, are designated to include Level C. Level A means knowledge and comprehension (identify, define, explain). Level B adds application and analysis (use the material, break problems down). Level C adds synthesis and evaluation — combining ideas, forming judgements, defending a course of action. In plain terms, IMA reserves the right to test every section at the deepest level: not 'what is a flexible budget?' but 'here is a messy company — build one, find the variance, and tell management what to do about it'. Hold that thought; it shapes the study strategy at the end of this article.

Part 1 — Financial Planning, Performance, and Analytics: The Full Syllabus

Part 1 is the operations engine of the qualification — how a company plans, costs, controls and measures itself from the inside. Candidates with cost-accounting or FP&A jobs often find its logic familiar; what surprises them is the technology section, the newest and most underestimated quarter of the paper.

SectionTitleWeightMain topics inside the section (per IMA CSO)
AExternal Financial Reporting Decisions15%Financial statements (balance sheet, income statement, changes in equity, cash flows, integrated reporting); recognition, measurement, valuation and disclosure — asset valuation, liabilities, equity transactions, revenue recognition, income measurement; major U.S. GAAP vs IFRS differences
BPlanning, Budgeting, and Forecasting20%Strategic planning; budgeting concepts; forecasting techniques (regression analysis, learning curves, expected value); budgeting methodologies (master budgets, project, activity-based, zero-based, continuous/rolling, flexible); annual profit plan and supporting schedules; top-level planning and analysis (pro forma statements, projections)
CPerformance Management20%Cost and variance measures (flexible budgets, standard costs, management by exception); responsibility centres and reporting segments (transfer pricing, segment reporting); performance measures (product/customer/business-unit profitability, ROI, residual income, KPIs, balanced scorecard)
DCost Management15%Measurement concepts (cost behaviour, absorption vs variable costing, joint and by-product costing); costing systems (job-order, process, activity-based, life-cycle); overhead costs and allocation, incl. service departments; supply chain management (lean, ERP, theory of constraints, capacity); business process improvement (value chain, ABM, cost of quality)
EInternal Controls15%Governance, risk and compliance (internal control structure, control risk, corporate governance, external audit requirements); systems controls and security measures (general and application controls, network controls, backup, business continuity planning)
FTechnology and Analytics15%Information systems (accounting systems, ERP, enterprise performance management); data governance (policies, data life cycle, breach controls); technology-enabled finance transformation (system development life cycle, process automation); data analytics (business intelligence, data mining, analytic tools, data visualisation)

Notice the architecture: Sections B and C together are 40% of Part 1, and they are really one subject viewed from two ends — B builds the plan, C measures what happened against it. Section D (costing) supplies the raw material both of them consume: you cannot budget or variance-analyse what you cannot cost. Section A is the language the whole part is written in, and Sections E and F are the modern control-and-data layer — 30% combined, heavily conceptual, and the sections self-studiers most often leave too late.

Part 2 — Strategic Financial Management: The Full Syllabus

Part 2 moves up a floor: from running the machine to financing it, valuing it and betting on its future. It reads like a finance director's job description, and its centre of gravity is unmistakable — one section, Decision Analysis, is worth a quarter of the entire exam.

SectionTitleWeightMain topics inside the section (per IMA CSO)
AFinancial Statement Analysis20%Basic analysis (common-size and common base-year statements); financial ratios (liquidity, leverage, activity, profitability, market); profitability analysis (income measurement, revenue, cost of sales, expense and variation analysis); special issues (foreign operations, inflation and changing prices, changes in accounting treatment, earnings quality)
BCorporate Finance20%Risk and return; long-term financial management (term structure of interest rates, financial instruments, cost of capital, valuation of instruments); raising capital (markets and regulation, IPOs, dividend policy and share repurchases, lease financing); working capital management (cash, receivables, inventory, short-term credit); corporate restructuring (M&A); international finance (exchange rates, transaction exposure, trade financing)
CDecision Analysis25%Cost/volume/profit analysis (breakeven, profit performance, multiple products); marginal analysis (sunk and opportunity costs, special orders, make vs buy, sell or process further, add/drop a segment, capacity); pricing (methodologies, target costing, demand elasticity, product life cycle, market structure)
DRisk Management10%Enterprise risk: types of risk; risk identification and assessment; risk mitigation strategies; managing risk
EInvestment Decisions10%Capital budgeting process (stages, incremental cash flows, tax considerations, evaluating uncertainty); capital investment analysis methods (NPV, IRR, payback, and comparing the methods)
FProfessional Ethics15%Business ethics (moral philosophies and values, ethical decision making); ethical considerations for management accounting professionals (IMA's Statement of Ethical Professional Practice, the fraud triangle, evaluating and resolving ethical issues); ethical considerations for the organisation (ethical culture and leadership, legal compliance, sustainability and social responsibility)

Part 2's trap is different from Part 1's. The syllabus looks smaller — fewer systems, less costing machinery — but the questions sit at the strategic end: ratio analysis feeding valuation, valuation feeding investment decisions, all of it wrapped in an ethics section worth 15%, which is more than Risk Management and Investment Decisions' neighbours expect and far too much to treat as a soft read in the final week.

What to Study First: A Weight-and-Linkage Strategy

Now the strategy. Two principles drive it. First, weight: study time should roughly track exam weight, because a 25% section returns two and a half times the marks of a 10% section for similar effort. Second, linkage: some sections are load-bearing — other sections are built on them — and those must come first regardless of weight. In Part 1, the chain runs A → D → B → C: you need financial-statement literacy (A) before costing (D) makes sense, costing before budgeting (B), and budgeting before variance and performance analysis (C) — which is why C, joint-heaviest at 20%, is studied fourth, not first. Then take E and F together as the conceptual block; their questions reward structured frameworks (COSO-style controls, data-governance concepts) more than calculation, and frameworks stick better when learned as a pair. In Part 2, start with A — ratio and statement analysis is the toolkit for everything after it — then C, the 25% giant, while your Part 1 costing is still warm (CVP and marginal analysis are Part 1's cost behaviour wearing a strategy hat). Follow with B, then E (NPV/IRR leans directly on B's cost of capital), and close with D and F as the conceptual pair — but do not exile ethics to the last fortnight: at 15%, F is Part 2's third-largest section, and its scenario questions punish candidates who memorised the IMA standards without practising how to apply them.

A realistic allocation for a working professional studying 8–10 hours a week — the pattern most of our Gulf students follow — is roughly: weight-proportional hours per section, plus a 20% loading on your weakest load-bearing section, plus a final quarter of total time reserved for mixed mock exams. The mocks are not optional garnish. With every section testable at Level C, the exam is ultimately a judgement test under time pressure, and judgement is only trained by answering integrated questions, reviewing why the wrong answers were wrong, and going again.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the CMA syllabus for Part 1?

Six sections: External Financial Reporting Decisions (15%), Planning, Budgeting & Forecasting (20%), Performance Management (20%), Cost Management (15%), Internal Controls (15%), and Technology & Analytics (15%) — per IMA's Content Specification Outlines.

What is the CMA Part 2 syllabus and its highest-weighted section?

Financial Statement Analysis (20%), Corporate Finance (20%), Decision Analysis (25%), Risk Management (10%), Investment Decisions (10%), and Professional Ethics (15%). Decision Analysis, at 25%, is the single heaviest section in either part.

Which CMA part should I take first — Part 1 or Part 2?

IMA allows either order. Most candidates take Part 1 first because its planning-costing-performance chain underpins Part 2's decision analysis. Candidates with strong corporate-finance jobs sometimes start with Part 2. Choose based on which syllabus your current work already rehearses.

What do Level A, B and C mean in the CMA syllabus?

They are IMA's coverage levels: A = knowledge and comprehension; B = adds application and analysis; C = adds synthesis and evaluation. All twelve sections across both parts are designated to include Level C, so every topic can be tested at judgement depth, not just recall.

How many study hours does each part need?

IMA-linked guidance commonly cites roughly 150–170 hours per part. Working professionals typically spread that over 4–6 months per part at 8–10 hours a week; the binding constraint is usually mock-exam practice, not reading time.

Does the CMA syllabus change often?

The section structure and weights in the current CSOs have been stable since the January 2020 outlines, but IMA updates content as standards evolve (GAAP/IFRS changes reach the exam about a year after their effective date). Always plan against the CSO version on imanet.org at the time you register.

Is Professional Ethics really 15% of Part 2?

Yes — per the official CSO. It covers business ethics, IMA's Statement of Ethical Professional Practice, the fraud triangle, and organisational ethics. At 15% it outweighs Risk Management and Investment Decisions, and its questions are scenario-based: learn the standards, then practise applying them.

Final Verdict

If the CMA syllabus has one sentence, it is this: Part 1 teaches you to run a company's finances from the inside — plan it, cost it, control it, measure it — and Part 2 teaches you to judge that company from the top — analyse it, finance it, invest in it, and behave ethically while doing so. The weights tell you where the marks live; the linkages tell you the order to claim them in; and Level C tells you that memorising definitions will not be enough for any of it. Study the map in that order and the syllabus stops being twelve sections and becomes what it actually is: one profession, described twice.
