How to Answer CMA Case-Based Questions (CBQs): A Step-by-Step Method

Quick answer

Spend about 30 minutes on each case. Scan the questions first, read the case for the figures those questions need, answer the question you are surest of first, and check units and rounding before you move on.

Never leave a question blank. IMA gives partial credit on case-based questions, and says there is no penalty for a wrong answer.

Since the September/October 2026 window, English CMA exams in the Middle East end each part with two case-based questions (CBQs). If you want the full story of the change, read our guide to CMA case-based questions vs essays. This guide is about the practical part: how to answer them.

We cover the method, how to handle each question type, two practice cases with full answers, and the mistakes that cost marks.

What the cases really test

Case-based questions test whether you can evaluate a set of data, make a judgement between alternatives and justify a course of action. That is closer to a real management accountant’s work than a single multiple-choice question.

The topics are the same as before. IMA says the case-based questions test the same topics at the same difficulty level as the essays. What changes is how you show your thinking: you select, drag and enter answers instead of writing paragraphs.

How a case is built

The case-based section of each CMA part
ElementWhat to expect
Cases per partTwo
Case lengthAbout 250 words, plus exhibits such as a financial schedule or chart
Questions per caseUp to seven, all about the same case
TimeAbout one hour for both cases, so about 30 minutes each
Time per questionAbout 4 to 6 minutes (30 minutes divided by 5 to 7 questions)
Weight25% of the part score, the same as the essays had
GateYou must answer at least 50% of the multiple-choice questions correctly to take the cases
Key point

All the questions in one case use the same business situation, so a figure you work out early may help again later. Check your first figure before you build on it.

The 6-step method for any case

  1. Reset and check the clockTake a few seconds between the multiple-choice section and the cases. If you finished the MCQs early, the time you have left carries over to the cases (Gleim). At about 1.5 minutes per MCQ, you could carry roughly 30 minutes of your three hours.
  2. Scan the questions firstSpend about a minute reading only the questions. Ask for each one: what topic is this testing, and is it a calculation, a choice or a match? Now you know what to hunt for.
  3. Read the case with a purposeRead it once. Mark the numbers and facts that your questions need, and ignore the rest.
  4. Start with the question you are surest ofAnswer in the order that suits you, not necessarily top to bottom. Early confidence saves time for the harder questions.
  5. Name the concept before you calculateSay it in a few words, for example “flexible budget variance” or “NPV at 10%”. Then decide which direction the working should go. This prevents long calculations that answer the wrong question.
  6. Check units and rounding, then move onConfirm the unit, the sign and the rounding instruction. If you are still on the first case after about 30 minutes, move to the second and return if time allows.
Read first or scan first?

Candidates differ on this, so treat it as a method to test, not a rule from IMA. We recommend scanning the questions first because they tell you what matters in the case. Whichever you pick, test it in timed practice and then use it every time, so it becomes automatic on exam day.

How to handle each question type

Gleim lists these question formats in case-based questions: numerical response, select from a list, drag and drop, multiple response and standard multiple choice. Each has its own trap.

Practice case 1: Part 1 (budgeting and variances)

Try it before opening the answers. This case is original, written by Knowledge Valley to match the format described above. It is shorter than a real case so that it fits on the page.

Practice case 1CMA PART 1 · PLANNING AND BUDGETING

Sahra Packaging planned to produce and sell 20,000 units this quarter. The budget allows a variable cost of $8 per unit and fixed costs of $50,000. Actual output was 22,000 units. Actual variable costs were $187,000 and actual fixed costs were $52,000.

1Numerical entry

What is the flexible budget total cost at 22,000 units, in dollars?

Show answer and method

Answer: 226000

Variable: 22,000 × $8 = $176,000. Add fixed costs of $50,000. Total: $226,000. Enter it without a dollar sign or comma.

2Numerical entry

Actual total cost was $239,000. What is the total variance against the flexible budget, in dollars? Enter a positive number.

Show answer and method

Answer: 13000 (unfavourable)

$239,000 − $226,000 = $13,000. Actual cost is higher than the flexible budget, so the variance is unfavourable.

3Select from a list

The static budget cost is $210,000. The $16,000 gap between the static budget and the flexible budget is mainly caused by:

  • Higher activity (volume)
  • Poor cost control
  • Price increases
Show answer and method

Answer: Higher activity (volume)

The static budget uses 20,000 units and the flexible budget uses 22,000. The 2,000 extra units × $8 = $16,000. The gap comes from output, not from cost control or prices.

4Drag and drop

Match each cost to its variance against the flexible budget. One option will not be used.

  • $11,000 unfavourable
  • $2,000 unfavourable
  • $16,000 unfavourable
CostVariance
Variable costsdrop here
Fixed costsdrop here
Show answer and method

Variable costs: $11,000 unfavourable. Fixed costs: $2,000 unfavourable. Unused: $16,000.

Variable: $187,000 − $176,000 = $11,000. Fixed: $52,000 − $50,000 = $2,000. Together they make $13,000, which matches question 2. That cross-check is a quick way to catch a slip. The $16,000 is the volume gap from question 3, not a cost variance.

Practice case 2: Part 2 (investment decision)

Practice case 2CMA PART 2 · CAPITAL BUDGETING

Dune Logistics (a fictional company) is considering a new warehouse system. It costs $100,000 today and is expected to save $40,000 a year in cash costs for three years. The company’s required rate of return is 10%.

1Numerical entry

What is the net present value (NPV) of the project, rounded to the nearest dollar? Enter a negative number with a minus sign if the NPV is negative.

Show answer and method

Answer: -526

The three-year annuity factor at 10% is 2.48685. Present value of savings = $40,000 × 2.48685 = $99,474. NPV = $99,474 − $100,000 = −$526.

2Select from a list

Based on NPV alone, the company should:

  • Accept the project
  • Reject the project
Show answer and method

Answer: Reject the project

A negative NPV means the project earns less than the required 10% return. Here the NPV is only slightly negative, so in real life managers would also look at risks and non-financial benefits. But on NPV alone the answer is reject.

3Multiple response

Select the TWO statements that are correct.

  • A. The payback period is 2.5 years
  • B. The payback period accounts for the time value of money
  • C. The project’s internal rate of return (IRR) is below 10%
  • D. The NPV would be positive at a 12% discount rate
Show answer and method

Answer: A and C

A: $100,000 ÷ $40,000 = 2.5 years. B is wrong: payback ignores the time value of money. C: the NPV at 10% is negative, so the IRR must be below 10% (it is about 9.7%). D is wrong: a higher discount rate makes the NPV more negative, not positive.

Watch out: rounding

In case 2, using a rounded annuity factor of 2.49 gives an NPV of −$400, not −$526. When the exam gives a rounding instruction, follow it. When it does not, keep full figures in your working and round only the final answer.

A simple practice routine

  1. Do one timed case after each topicSet a 30-minute timer and use the same screen format as the exam. Doing it on paper does not train drag-and-drop or drop-down questions.
  2. Keep an error logFor every wrong answer, tag it as a concept error, a reading error, a calculation error or a format error (for example, a wrong rounding or a missed unused option).
  3. Fix the biggest tag firstAfter two or three weeks you will see the pattern. If most errors are calculation slips, drill the calculation. If most are reading errors, slow down on step 3.
  4. Finish with full mock examsMock exams give you the real pressure of moving from 100 MCQs to two cases, and show whether your time plan holds.

Keep your multiple-choice practice going alongside. You must answer at least 50% of the multiple-choice questions correctly to take the cases, so the two sections support each other.

FAQs

How do I start a CMA case-based question?

Take a breath, then scan the questions before you read the case, so you know which numbers and facts to look for. Read the case with those questions in mind, mark the key figures, and answer the item you are surest of first. The method above sets this out step by step.

How much time should I spend on each case-based question?

About 30 minutes each. You have about one hour for the two cases in a part, and each case has up to seven questions, so that is roughly four to six minutes per question. If you are still on the first case after about 30 minutes, move on and come back if time allows.

Should I read the case first or the questions first?

Both approaches are taught. We suggest scanning the questions first because they tell you what to look for in the case, then reading the case once with a purpose. Test both in timed practice and keep the one that gives you more correct answers, then use it every time.

Do I type dollar signs or commas in numerical answers?

No. Gleim says you won’t enter dollar signs or commas in numerical answers, and that the exam gives rounding instructions as needed. Read the instruction under each question before you type.

Is there partial credit on case-based questions?

Yes. IMA says case-based questions give partial credit, as the essays did, and that there is no penalty for wrong answers. You earn points for each question you answer correctly, so never leave a question blank.

Can I use spare time from the multiple-choice section on the cases?

Yes. Gleim says that if you finish the multiple-choice section early, the time you have left carries over to the case-based section. At about 1.5 minutes a question, the 100 MCQs take about 150 of your 180 minutes, so you could carry roughly 30 minutes into the cases.

How long is a case, and how many questions does it have?

Gleim describes each case as about 250 words, with exhibits such as financial information or charts, and up to seven questions. You get two cases after the multiple-choice section, and about one hour for both. IMA has not published a detailed scoring method, so we do not guess at one.

What is the best way to practise case-based questions?

Do one timed case after every topic you finish, in the same format as the exam. Then log every mistake as a concept, reading, calculation or format error. After two or three weeks, the log shows you which type of mistake to fix first.
